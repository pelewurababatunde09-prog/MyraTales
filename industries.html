A Car for the Great Multitude

Henry Ford didn't want to build a luxury object. He wanted something so simple and cheap that an ordinary working family could actually own one — and the idea that finally made that possible came, of all places, from a meatpacking plant.

Part Four — Mass Production

VI. A Car for the Great Multitude

Detroit, Michigan | 1 October 1908

Well established

By the turn of the century, dozens of small firms on both sides of the Atlantic were hand-building automobiles for the wealthy, each car essentially a custom commission assembled from parts fitted individually by skilled machinists. Henry Ford, a former chief engineer at the Edison Illuminating Company who had already founded and abandoned one failed car company before starting the Ford Motor Company in 1903, wanted something different: not a luxury object, but a vehicle simple and cheap enough that, in his own words at the time, "no man making a good salary will be unable to own one."

That vehicle, the Model T, was introduced on 1 October 1908, built initially by hand at Ford's Piquette Avenue plant in Detroit. Demand outstripped hand production almost immediately — only eleven cars were completed in the Model T's entire first month — and Ford broke ground on a far larger, purpose-built factory in the Detroit suburb of Highland Park, which opened on 1 January 1910. The Model T itself was engineered for durability over elegance: a simple 20-horsepower four-cylinder engine, a tough vanadium steel alloy chassis rare in cars of that price range, and a planetary transmission simple enough for a farmer with no mechanical training to learn to drive and repair it on rutted, often unpaved American country roads.

At its 1908 launch the Model T sold for $850, comparable to roughly two to three years of an average American factory worker's wages at the time — still well beyond a genuinely mass market, and still built the old way, one car assembled from start to finish by a single team before the next was begun.

Part Four — Mass Production

VII. The Line That Never Stops

Highland Park, Michigan | 1913 – 1914

Core timeline established, one affordability statistic is a modern economic estimate

The idea that finally closed the gap between the Model T's price and Ford's promise of a car for "the great multitude" did not originate in the auto industry at all. Ford engineers borrowed it, by their own later admission, from an altogether less glamorous source: the disassembly lines of Chicago and Cincinnati meatpacking plants, where overhead trolleys carried carcasses past stationary workers, each performing one repeated cut before passing the animal on to the next station. Ford's insight was to run that logic in reverse — instead of taking a product apart piece by piece as it moved past fixed workers, build one up the same way.

The first trial ran on a small scale in early 1913, assembling flywheel magnetos rather than whole cars: the job was broken into twenty-nine separate operations performed by twenty-nine men spaced along a moving belt, and average assembly time for a single magneto dropped from twenty minutes to five almost immediately. Encouraged, Ford's engineers scaled the idea up. On 7 October 1913, Highland Park switched its chassis assembly to a continuously moving line, with a motor and rope literally dragging each chassis frame past stationary workers and stacked parts; assembly time for a single chassis fell from roughly twelve and a half hours to about six almost overnight. On 1 December 1913, the moving line was extended to cover the entire car, not just the chassis. By early 1914, with the crude rope-and-slide mechanism replaced by a mechanized chain conveyor, three fused, continuous production lines could turn out a finished Model T roughly every ninety-three minutes — an improvement of close to ninety percent over the old hand-built method, and at the line's later peak, a completed Model T was rolling off Highland Park's floor approximately every twenty-four seconds.

Rare detail

The line's relentless, repetitive pace was punishing enough that by late 1913 worker turnover at Highland Park reportedly reached 380 percent annually — meaning, in effect, Ford had to hire nearly four full replacement workforces over the course of a single year just to keep every station staffed. His solution, announced on 5 January 1914, was to roughly double the standard wage to five dollars for an eight-hour day, up from a prior rate historians place at somewhere around $2.30–$2.60 for a longer working day. The move was widely covered as an act of generosity; internally, Ford and his executives framed it just as much as a turnover-reduction strategy, and observed, not incidentally, that better-paid workers made better customers for the very cars they were building.

The economics reshaped the entire market within a decade. As assembly costs collapsed, Ford repeatedly cut the Model T's price: from $850 in 1908 to $490 by 1914, then to as low as $260 by 1924. One widely cited modern economic analysis, using a "time price" method that measures cost in hours of labor rather than dollars, calculates that the price fell by roughly 88 percent in labor-hour terms between 1909 and 1922 — a specific economic estimate, not a period-recorded figure, but one consistent with the well-documented combination of falling prices and Ford's rising wages. On 4 June 1924, the ten-millionth Model T rolled off the Highland Park line. Ford Motor Company would go on to build more than fifteen million Model Ts in total before production finally ended in 1927.

This chapter draws on company archives, contemporary press accounts, and published economic analysis of the Model T era, including the "time price" methodology used by economist Gale Pooley. Wage figures for pre-1914 Ford workers vary somewhat between historical sources and are presented here as an approximate range rather than an exact figure. Dates and figures reflect the ranges most widely cited in current research as of 2026.
