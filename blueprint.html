Part One — Steam

I. A Wheel That Runs on Fire

Paris, France | 23 October 1769 – 2 October 1804

Core events established, one incident unconfirmed

Where the last story left off, the wheel had already been rolling for roughly fifty-five centuries — pulled by oxen across Sumerian mud brick, spun into spokes on the Eurasian steppe, bolted with iron tires onto Roman freight wagons. What it had never done, in all that time, was move under its own power.

That changed in a Paris workshop on 23 October 1769, when a French artillery officer named Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot wheeled a strange three-wheeled contraption in front of General Jean-Baptiste de Gribeauval and a cluster of skeptical army officers. Cugnot had spent nearly six years teaching himself steam mechanics from German technical literature while stationed with the artillery, convinced the same principle that drove a steam pump could haul a cannon without a team of horses. He called his machine a fardier à vapeur — literally a "steam dray," after the heavy horse-drawn carts normally used to move artillery barrels. The English press covering the demonstration, not quite sure what to call it, settled on the word "locomotive."

The little 1769 prototype barely qualified as transportation: it needed twelve to fifteen minutes of standing still to build enough boiler pressure for a single short burst of movement, topping out around 2 miles per hour before it had to stop and build pressure all over again. It was, nonetheless, unmistakably self-propelled, and the army was intrigued enough to fund a full-size follow-up. Built at the Strasbourg Arsenal and formally presented to King Louis XV on 22 April 1770, the second fardier weighed roughly 2.5 tonnes and was designed to haul four tons of cargo at nearly 5 miles per hour — a target it never once hit in practice. Its front wheel carried the entire copper boiler and drive mechanism in a top-heavy arrangement that made the whole vehicle dangerously unstable on anything but a perfectly flat surface.

Rare detail

According to an account that only surfaced in writing in 1801, thirty years after the fact, Cugnot lost control of this second fardier sometime around 1771 and drove it straight into a stone wall at the Paris Arsenal, flattening it — arguably the first automobile accident in history, and, if the accompanying legend of his arrest for dangerous driving is true, arguably also the first traffic citation. No contemporary document records the incident, so historians treat it as plausible but unconfirmed folklore rather than settled fact.

The project quietly died anyway, undone less by the wall than by shifting political winds: Cugnot's patron, the Duc de Choiseul, fell from royal favor in December 1770, and funding for the fardier dried up with him. Cugnot lived long enough to see the French Revolution strip him of the modest royal pension Louis XV had granted him, forcing him into a poor exile in Brussels. Napoleon Bonaparte restored the pension shortly before Cugnot's death in Paris on 2 October 1804 — meaning the man usually credited with building the world's first self-propelled land vehicle died just as the century that would make his invention a household object was about to begin. His original 1770 fardier survives today at the Musée des Arts et Métiers in Paris, widely credited as the oldest self-propelled mechanical vehicle still in existence anywhere in the world.

Part One — Steam to Spark

II. The Engine Before the Car

Paris & Cologne | 1860 – 1876

Well established

Steam power had an obvious flaw for anything meant to move on demand: it took real time to build a head of pressure before the vehicle could go anywhere at all, which was tolerable for a train departing on a schedule and hopeless for anyone who simply wanted to leave the house. The fix would not come from a better boiler. It came from moving the fire inside the cylinder itself, igniting fuel directly against the piston rather than boiling water to push it.

The first person to build such an engine in meaningful numbers was a Belgian-born engineer working in Paris, Jean Joseph Étienne Lenoir, who in 1860 patented a two-stroke engine that burned ordinary coal gas — the same gas used to light city streetlamps — and ignited it mid-stroke with an electric spark from a device called a Ruhmkorff coil, itself adapted from telegraph technology. Lenoir's engine was crude and thermally inefficient, consuming enormous volumes of gas for the power it produced, but it worked reliably enough to be sold commercially, and Lenoir himself bolted a version of it into an experimental carriage — among the earliest vehicles known to have been propelled by internal combustion, though it never moved beyond the demonstration stage.

In Cologne, a young traveling grocery salesman named Nikolaus August Otto became fascinated by reports of the Lenoir engine and, with his brother, built an unauthorized copy in 1861, applying for his own patent on an improved, liquid-fueled version. The Prussian government rejected the application. Otto pressed on anyway, teaming up in 1864 with the industrialist's son Eugen Langen to found N. A. Otto & Cie in Cologne — among the first companies built specifically to manufacture internal combustion engines, and the ancestor of the modern industrial giant Deutz AG. It took Otto a further decade of failed experiments, funding crises, and a shattered first working relationship with his own brother before he perfected what became known as the Otto cycle: intake, compression, power, exhaust, built in working form by 1876 and patented shortly after. Compressing the fuel-air mixture before ignition, rather than igniting it at normal atmospheric pressure the way Lenoir had, dramatically increased the engine's efficiency and, for the first time, made an internal combustion engine practical enough to seriously consider putting inside a vehicle.

Rare detail

Otto's patent did not survive long. In 1886, German courts researching prior art discovered that a little-known French engineer named Alphonse Beau de Rochas had already described the exact four-stroke principle in an obscure, privately published 1862 pamphlet — without ever building a working engine to prove it. Otto's patent was voided as a result, a defeat he reportedly took as a personal insult for the rest of his life. He died in Cologne on 26 January 1891, having built the engine that worked but lost the legal claim to having invented the idea behind it. Other engineers, including Munich watchmaker Christian Reithmann, also later claimed to have anticipated parts of the same principle — a reminder that, as with the wheel itself, the four-stroke cycle's origin was less a single lightning-bolt invention than a case of several people converging on the same idea from different directions.

One of the engineers who cut his teeth working for Otto's company in the 1870s was a young German named Gottlieb Daimler, hired as technical director in 1872 alongside a talented young draftsman, Wilhelm Maybach. Neither man is the subject of the next chapter's opening scene, but both spent the 1870s absorbing everything Otto's four-stroke cycle could teach them — knowledge they would soon put to a use Otto himself never fully embraced: making the engine small, light, and fast enough to actually go somewhere.
