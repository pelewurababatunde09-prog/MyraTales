Part Four — Mass Production

VIII. The World Fills With Cars

United States & Beyond | 1908 – 1927

Assembly line's influence well established; the black-paint drying-time claim is a debunked myth

The assembly line's real legacy was never confined to Ford, or even to cars. Within a generation the same continuous-flow logic Ford had borrowed from meatpackers spread outward to appliance makers, textile mills, and factories across Europe and Asia, becoming the default template for twentieth-century mass manufacturing of almost every kind. Historians have traced its later influence as far as postwar Japanese manufacturing, where firms studying Highland Park's methods in the following decades adapted and refined them into production philosophies that would eventually be studied and re-exported right back to Detroit.

For the car itself, mass production meant a real shift in what a car was allowed to look like. Ford's insistence on standardized components famously narrowed the Model T's paint options for much of its run to a single color, black — but not, as is commonly repeated, because black dried faster than other paints. Dedicated research into Ford's own engineering records and employee accounts, notably by economist Trent Boggess, found no support for the drying-time explanation at all: the Model T was actually available in several colors, including red, blue, green, and gray, for its first five years, with black only becoming the sole option from 1914 to 1925. The real reasons were cost and durability — black paints of the era, particularly those containing asphaltum, held up better against damp and wear than the alternatives — combined with the simple production advantage of eliminating color changeovers on a fast-moving line. The popular version of the story hardened, decades later, into one of the most quoted lines in automotive history, Ford's own remark that a customer could have a car painted any color he wanted, so long as it was black — a real quote, describing a real policy, just not for the reason usually attached to it. It was a trade-off customers of the assembly-line era, unlike the wealthy clientele of the earlier hand-built Benz and Daimler cars, were, for the most part, willing to accept in exchange for a price they could actually afford.

Epilogue

IX. Handing Back the Wheel

1920s – Today

Well established

By the time the Model T ended production in 1927, the automobile had already completed the transformation this story set out to trace: a wheel, invented by an anonymous Mesopotamian potter roughly five thousand five hundred years ago to shape wet clay, had been reinvented as a spoke, then a chariot, then a Roman freight axle, then, in barely a century and a half, an engine-driven machine capable of carrying an ordinary family faster and farther than any human being had ever traveled unaided in the whole prior span of the species' existence.

Everything that follows this point — the closed steel body, the automatic transmission, the interstate highway system, the safety belt and the airbag, the hybrid drivetrain, and whatever comes after the internal combustion engine currently under the hood — belongs to a chapter still being written by people much closer to it than any historian. That part of the story is deliberately left here, at the threshold, for someone else to tell.

This chapter draws on company archives, contemporary press accounts, and published automotive history associated with Ford Motor Company production practices between 1908 and 1927. The claim that Model T paint was limited to black for faster drying time has been corrected here against dedicated historical research; the color-restriction policy itself, and the associated Ford quotation, are accurately reported. Dates and figures reflect the ranges most widely cited in current research as of 2026.
