A Peek Into the Future

Two rival German workshops, sixty miles apart, both finish the same idea without ever meeting. A wife takes her husband's invention on a drive he never approved. And on the other side of an ocean, American hands build a car of their own — and learn, almost immediately, what a car can cost a stranger on the street.

Part Two — Ignition

III. Two Workshops, One Idea

Mannheim & Cannstatt, Germany | 1885 – 1886

Core patents and dates established, exact Reitwagen date unconfirmed

By complete coincidence — the two men never met and worked barely sixty miles apart without either fully realizing how close the other had come — two separate German engineers spent the same year, 1885, independently finishing the machine that would define the next century of transportation.

In Mannheim, Karl Benz, who had completed his own two-stroke gas engine on New Year's Eve 1879 and patented it the following June, had spent the mid-1880s refining a light, reliable four-stroke engine and mounting it not into a converted horse carriage but into a purpose-built three-wheeled vehicle, designed from the ground up around the engine rather than adapted afterward. He filed for a patent on this complete vehicle, and German patent number 37435 was granted to Benz on 29 January 1886 — widely credited as the world's first patent for a complete, purpose-built automobile, and the reason the vehicle is still known today as the Benz Patent-Motorwagen. He unveiled it publicly on the streets of Mannheim on 3 July 1886. It seated two, weighed about 265 kilograms, produced roughly two-thirds of a single horsepower from a single horizontal cylinder, and cost 600 imperial German marks to build — a little over $150 at the time, or roughly $5,400 in today's money.

Sixty miles away in Cannstatt, near Stuttgart, Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach had taken the opposite approach: rather than build a vehicle around an engine, they set out to build the smallest, fastest-spinning gasoline engine physically possible, one they could later drop into almost anything — a boat, a stagecoach, or nothing bigger than a bicycle. They patented their compact single-cylinder design on 3 April 1885, nicknaming it the Standuhr, or "grandfather clock" engine, for its resemblance to the tall pendulum clocks common in German parlors. That November, they fitted a smaller version onto a wooden, iron-plated two-wheeled frame stabilized by a pair of small outrigger wheels, and on a night that history has not pinned down to an exact date, Daimler's teenage son Paul rode it roughly three kilometers from Cannstatt to Unterturkheim, reaching about 12 kilometers per hour. The Reitwagen, as it became known, is now recognized as one of the earliest known internal-combustion motorcycles — built, notably, not because Daimler and Maybach particularly wanted to invent the motorcycle, but simply because a two-wheeled test bed was the cheapest way to prove their new engine actually worked on the road.

Rare detail

Daimler and Maybach's first four-wheeled vehicle was built almost furtively. On 8 March 1886, the pair bought an ordinary American-style horse coach from the Stuttgart coachbuilder Wilhelm Wimpff & Sohn, telling curious neighbors it was simply a birthday present for Mrs. Daimler. Maybach quietly removed the space meant for a horse's harness and installed an enlarged, 1.5-horsepower grandfather clock engine with a belt drive to the rear wheels — producing, in effect, a motorized carriage disguised the entire time as an ordinary gift.

Neither Benz nor Daimler used the word "automobile," which did not enter common usage for another decade, and neither man, at the time, had any idea the other's workshop existed. History has settled the priority question narrowly in Benz's favor, since his design was patented as a complete, purpose-built vehicle rather than an engine subsequently fitted to something else — but for years afterward, both companies, and their respective heirs, competed fiercely over the title of having built the first true car, a rivalry that only formally ended in 1926 when the two firms merged to form Daimler-Benz.

Part Two — Ignition

IV. Bertha's Drive

Mannheim to Pforzheim, Germany | 5 August 1888

Core journey well documented, some "world's first" claims are commemorative tradition

A patented, working automobile was not, on its own, a business. In its first two years on the market the Patent-Motorwagen sold almost nothing: it was loud, it was strange, and every public demonstration Karl Benz gave seemed to end in mechanical trouble or public mockery rather than orders. What actually rescued the company was not an engineering fix. It was Karl's wife, Bertha Ringer Benz, acting almost entirely on her own initiative and, by most accounts, without her husband's advance permission.

Before dawn on 5 August 1888, Bertha loaded her two teenage sons, fifteen-year-old Eugen and fourteen-year-old Richard, into Patent-Motorwagen No. 3, left a note on the kitchen table saying only that she was taking the boys to visit their grandmother, and drove out of Mannheim toward Pforzheim, her childhood home roughly 105 kilometers away — a distance many times longer than any automobile had previously traveled in one continuous trip. She had no map worth the name and largely followed cart tracks and existing wagon roads. Along the way she improvised solutions to problems no one had yet had reason to solve: she used a long hatpin to clear a clogged fuel line, wrapped a bare, short-circuiting ignition wire with a strip of her own garter as improvised insulation, and, when the wooden brake blocks wore thin on a long downhill grade near Wiesloch, walked into a village cobbler's shop and asked him to nail leather onto the brake blocks — an act widely credited, across multiple historical accounts, as the invention of the world's first brake lining. She also stopped at a Wiesloch pharmacy, the Stadt-Apotheke, to buy ligroin, a cleaning solvent sold over the counter, because it was the only fuel similar enough to gasoline available anywhere along the route; the pharmacy is today commemorated as the world's first filling station — a title that reflects long-standing local and historical tradition rather than a formally contested technical record. On steep uphill stretches, with the single-speed engine unable to cope, her sons climbed down and pushed.

Rare detail

Bertha Benz was, by multiple contemporary and biographical accounts, more than a passenger in her husband's invention. Family financial records suggest her dowry helped bankroll the Patent-Motorwagen's development in the first place, and she is credited with pushing Karl toward design refinements he had been reluctant to make. Because women in the Grand Duchy of Baden were barred from university study at the time, her engineering contributions went largely uncredited in her own lifetime and were only widely recognized starting in the early 21st century.

Bertha and her sons reached Pforzheim that evening, dusty but unharmed, having covered roughly 180 kilometers round trip once the return journey was included. The stir she caused in every village along the route — residents had never seen a horseless carriage, let alone one driven by a woman — generated exactly the publicity Karl Benz's demonstrations had failed to produce on their own. Orders followed. The trip is commemorated today by the Bertha Benz Memorial Route, a signposted 194-kilometer heritage road officially recognized by German authorities in 2008, and by a biennial vintage car rally that retraces her path. Roughly twenty-five Patent-Motorwagen units were built in total between 1886 and 1894 — a tiny number by later standards, but the first automobile production run in history all the same.

Part Three — Crossing the Ocean

V. The Race Across the Atlantic

Springfield, Massachusetts & London | 1893 – 1896

Well established

Word of the German engines crossed the Atlantic quickly, but it took American hands to build the first gasoline car actually constructed on U.S. soil. In Springfield, Massachusetts, brothers Charles and J. Frank Duryea — who had earned local fame as the "Bicycle Brothers" for their custom cycle work — spent their evenings at the Springfield public library teaching themselves internal combustion theory from whatever technical journals they could find. Charles sketched the design before an ongoing sibling dispute sent him back to Illinois in a temper, leaving Frank to actually build the thing: a used $70 horse buggy fitted with a single-cylinder, four-horsepower gasoline engine of Frank's own construction, a spray-type carburetor reportedly inspired by the mechanism of a perfume atomizer, and a friction transmission. On 21 September 1893, Frank drove it down the streets of Springfield in what the next morning's Springfield Morning Union described, with evident bewilderment, as a common top carriage gliding by with no shafts and no horse attached. It broke down after a few hundred feet with a worn bearing, was repaired on the spot, and covered roughly half a mile more before the day was through.

The Duryea brothers went on to found the Duryea Motor Wagon Company in 1895, the first American firm organized specifically to build and sell automobiles, and Frank drove one of their cars to victory in America's first motor race, the Chicago Times-Herald race of November 1895, at an average speed of roughly 7.5 miles per hour through snow — beating, among other entrants, a car built under license from Benz's own German designs. But automobiles brought a hazard no one had yet had to legislate for. On 30 May 1896, a Duryea car being driven through New York City collided with a bicyclist named Henry Wells, breaking his leg in what is widely regarded as the first traffic collision recorded in the United States; the driver spent a night in jail before being released.

Britain's reckoning came a few months later and proved far graver. On 17 August 1896, a 44-year-old woman named Bridget Driscoll was struck and killed by a demonstration car belonging to the Anglo-French Motor Car Company in the grounds of London's Crystal Palace, while attending a charity fair with her teenage daughter. The car was reportedly traveling at only about 4 miles per hour, a speed limit that had in fact just been raised to 14 miles per hour by an act of Parliament passed weeks earlier. At the inquest, the coroner expressed hope that Driscoll's death would be the last of its kind — a wish that history would not grant.

Rare detail

Driscoll was not, in fact, the first person killed in connection with a motor vehicle. That grim distinction is usually given to Mary Ward, an Irish scientist and astronomer, who was thrown from an experimental steam carriage built by her cousins near Birr, Ireland, on 31 August 1869 and died from her injuries — the difference being that Ward died as a passenger riding in an early motor vehicle, while Driscoll, twenty-seven years later, was the first pedestrian killed by one.
